# Owner stories (fictional)

Twenty fictional owner interviews (product and brand names replaced by generic words) from B2B IT-maintenance and office-equipment service businesses, written as an
owner would explain the business at the start of a workflow diagnosis. Company sizes, volumes and incidents are
illustrative. They are the source of the synthetic cases C01 to C20 and the demo conversations; they are not in the
knowledge base, because the cases already carry them.

## S01. "The same printer problem reaches three people, and we send two technicians."

*Owner of a 14-person PC and printer maintenance company serving approximately 90 offices and schools.*

"We have nine field technicians, two office coordinators, one salesperson, my wife handling accounts, and me. Most customers pay a monthly maintenance fee, although replacement parts are sometimes extra.

"Customers are supposed to call the office, but long-standing customers contact their usual technician directly. Others send messages to me on a chat app. The office writes requests into an a spreadsheet and posts assignments in our technicians' group chat. If someone calls a technician directly, we usually find out later.

"Last Tuesday, a teacher called our office about a printer. Another teacher messaged the technician who normally visits that school. The office assigned someone else because the regular technician looked busy. Both went there. The descriptions were different—one said 'printer offline,' the other said 'cannot print from classroom PC'—so nobody realized they were the same incident.

"The opposite also happens: everyone assumes someone else is handling a message, and nobody goes. I cannot reliably tell customers whether a request is received, assigned, or finished without calling around.

"We tried asking customers to use an online form, but many continued calling. I need us to capture those requests without making customers change how they contact us. I also need to distinguish a duplicate report from two separate faults at the same school."

## S02. "Our daily schedule falls apart before the technicians reach their first appointment."

*Owner of a 22-person field maintenance company covering customers across a city and its surrounding towns.*

"We have 15 technicians on the road. Our dispatcher prepares tomorrow's schedule at around five each afternoon using a spreadsheet and an online map. She groups visits geographically and generally keeps the same technician with the same customers.

"By nine the next morning, several customers have called with urgent problems. The dispatcher phones technicians individually to rearrange their routes. Sometimes a technician is already halfway to a customer when we change the plan.

"A nearby technician is not always the right person. One carries copier parts but does not handle server problems. Another knows a particular school's network, but his van may not contain the required equipment. Some sites only allow work during lunch or after classes. One customer insists on calling every printing problem urgent, while another quietly waits even when an entire department is affected.

"Last week, we redirected a technician to an urgent visit, then discovered that the customer's authorized contact would not arrive until the afternoon. We lost the original appointment and still could not complete the urgent job.

"We record arrival times inconsistently, so I cannot separate driving, waiting, and repair time. I want a workable way to revise the schedule throughout the day, with the dispatcher retaining control when customer relationships or unusual circumstances matter."

## S03. "We promise a monthly visit, but nobody agrees on what that visit should accomplish."

*Owner of a school-focused maintenance provider with 11 employees and 38 school contracts.*

"Our contracts generally include regular preventive visits and support when something breaks. At the start of the month, our administrator puts each school into a calendar. Technicians visit, ask the school contact whether anything is wrong, inspect some equipment, and obtain a signature.

"The problem is that 'preventive maintenance' means different things to different technicians. One checks printer rollers and storage capacity. Another fixes whatever the teachers mention. Another mainly collects the signature because he has several emergency calls waiting.

"Sometimes we visit a school on Monday for an urgent repair and return on Thursday for its scheduled maintenance. The school asks why we did not do everything on Monday. Sometimes combining the visits would have been sensible; sometimes the urgent repair left no time for the planned checks.

"We also arrive when the computer room is occupied or the person holding the key is absent. Our report says 'visit completed,' but some equipment was never inspected.

"I do not want to cancel visits that we have promised. I want to know what each contract requires, what was actually checked, and whether an earlier visit fulfilled any of those obligations. Right now, a signed sheet tells me very little about the maintenance itself."

## S04. "We have enough toner overall, but the right cartridge is never in the right place."

*Owner of a printer rental and consumables company managing approximately 650 devices.*

"We rent printers and copiers to offices and schools. Toner is included in most agreements. Customers call when the machine shows a warning, and our office checks a spreadsheet before arranging delivery.

"The warehouse spreadsheet might show five cartridges, but two are in technicians' vans, one is reserved for an installation, and one was delivered yesterday without being recorded. The office tells the customer we have stock, and then the technician cannot find it.

"Customers usually describe the device as 'the printer upstairs.' We ask for a photograph, but sometimes they photograph a different printer. Similar-looking models use different cartridges, so we occasionally deliver the wrong item.

"We tried leaving spare toner with customers. That reduced emergency deliveries at some sites, but other sites accumulated several spares because different staff members kept ordering. We do not have a dependable record of customer-held stock.

"When a customer says they have run out, I cannot tell whether they genuinely have no usable toner, have misplaced the spare, or are reacting to an early warning.

"I want fewer emergency trips and less unused stock, but I would not trust automatic ordering until our device information, stock movements, and customer-held quantities are more reliable."

## S05. "Every month, invoicing turns into a search for printer meter photographs."

*Owner of a managed print business with approximately 420 devices on usage-based contracts.*

"Our rental agreements usually have a base monthly fee, an included page allowance, and an excess charge. Some calculate black-and-white and color pages separately. A few customers have shared allowances across multiple machines.

"During the last week of the month, our administrator requests meter readings. Some devices report remotely, some customers send photographs, and technicians collect the rest during visits. She enters everything into a spreadsheet and calculates the charges before sending the invoice information to accounts.

"The difficult cases consume most of the time. A customer sends last month's photograph again. A machine is replaced halfway through the billing period. A counter photograph is blurred. Another customer sends a total count, but we need separate color and monochrome counts.

"We had a dispute because an opening reading from a replacement device was entered against the old device. The calculation looked valid, but the usage was wrong.

"Our administrator knows which customers have special terms because she remembers the negotiations. Those exceptions are not all represented consistently in the spreadsheet.

"I want routine invoices prepared with less manual work, but questionable readings should come back for review. Automatically sending an invoice based on a suspicious reading would create more work than it saves."

## S06. "We only discover whether equipment is covered after we have repaired it."

*Owner of an 18-person company providing mixed PC, printer, and network maintenance.*

"We have around 110 maintenance customers. A customer calls, the coordinator checks the company name, and we dispatch someone. That sounds straightforward, but the customer being under contract does not mean every device or every kind of work is included.

"One contract covers listed PCs and printers. Another includes general office support but excludes parts. Some include network equipment, while others do not. Customers buy additional devices without telling us, and staff move equipment between branches.

"A technician recently repaired a laptop at a contracted customer's office. Later, we discovered that it was an employee's personal laptop. Another time, we replaced a component that should have required a separate quotation. The customer said nobody had mentioned a charge, so I waived it.

"We have device lists attached to some contracts, but they are outdated. Serial numbers are missing, and the customer's names for equipment do not match ours.

"If technicians call the office before every small action, jobs become slow. If they use their own judgment, we treat similar situations differently.

"I need enough information before dispatch to identify likely coverage, and a clear approval process when the actual work turns out to be outside the agreement. We still need flexibility for small goodwill repairs."

## S07. "A ten-minute diagnosis turns into a two-week repair because nobody owns the parts follow-up."

*Owner of a nine-person repair and maintenance company specializing in business PCs and laptops.*

"We support small companies under contract and also accept individual business repairs. A technician diagnoses the fault and sends the office a message with the required part. The office asks suppliers for availability and prepares a quote. Once the customer approves, we place the order.

"The problem is everything between those steps. The technician may identify the laptop series without recording the exact submodel. The supplier asks a question, the administrator forwards it, and the technician is already at another site. The repair sits there.

"We have shelves labeled 'waiting for approval,' 'waiting for parts,' and 'ready,' but moving a laptop physically does not always update our spreadsheet. Customers call for progress, and the office has to inspect the shelf and search email.

"Last month, a customer approved a repair by replying to the technician's message. The office never saw the approval, so the part was not ordered for four days.

"We also miss supplier return deadlines when a replacement part does not solve the problem. Loaner laptops become another tracking issue.

"I want each repair to have a clear next action and responsible person. I am less worried about making the diagnosis faster than about the days when nothing happens and nobody notices."

## S08. "We send technicians for problems we could solve remotely, but remote support can also waste time."

*Owner of a 12-person IT support company serving approximately 75 small businesses.*

"Most requests involve office PCs, email, shared folders, printers, or business applications. Our administrator answers the phone and asks whether the customer wants a visit. Usually they say yes, so we arrange one.

"Some of those visits are simple: selecting the correct printer, reconnecting a mapped drive, or restarting a stalled application. We could handle them remotely. But we do not have a consistent first assessment.

"When a technician tries remote support, another problem appears. The remote tool may not be installed, the computer may have no internet access, or the person on the phone may not have permission to approve access. We spend 25 minutes trying to connect and then dispatch someone anyway.

"One customer was frustrated because three different staff members asked them to repeat the same symptoms. Another asked us not to connect during working hours because sensitive documents were open.

"We have an approved remote support tool, but customers have different access arrangements. Some require someone on-site to confirm every session.

"I want us to identify which requests are suitable for remote work and carry the diagnostic notes into the site visit if it fails. I do not want 'try remote support first' to become an extra delay for an obvious hardware or connectivity problem."

## S09. "School holidays are our busiest period, and we discover missing information on installation day."

*Owner of a 17-person company combining school maintenance with computer-room installation projects.*

"During term time, we maintain existing equipment. During school holidays, we replace PCs, prepare computer rooms, move printers, and install software. Several schools want everything completed within the same two or three weeks.

"The salesperson visits the school and prepares a quote. Once it is accepted, purchasing orders the equipment, and the technical lead prepares a deployment plan. Unfortunately, the site survey is usually a mixture of photographs, handwritten notes, and messages.

"On installation day, technicians discover that a room has fewer working network ports than expected, the furniture has moved, or the school has not provided the required software information. Old PCs may contain files that staff expected us to transfer, although data migration was never discussed.

"Last summer, we prepared dozens of PCs with one configuration, then learned that a particular classroom required different software. We had to reopen and reconfigure them.

"Meanwhile, our maintenance customers still expect normal response times, so I keep pulling technicians away from projects.

"I need better readiness checks before we commit the installation team, and visibility into both project work and ordinary support. A calendar saying 'School A installation' does not show whether the site, equipment, accounts, and customer decisions are actually ready."

## S10. "We use subcontractors to cover distant customers, but I cannot see what happened until there is a complaint."

*Owner of a regional maintenance company with eight employees and a network of external technicians.*

"We have customers with branches outside our normal service area. Rather than employ someone in every city, we use local service partners.

"When a branch reports a fault, our coordinator sends the subcontractor the address and symptoms by message. The partner accepts the job, visits, and sends a short completion message. We pay them later based on their invoice.

"The quality of reporting varies enormously. Some send photographs, device details, and a clear explanation. Others say only 'done.' We then have to prepare our own customer report from that information.

"A partner recently replaced a part without getting approval for the cost. The branch employee said it was fine, but the customer's head office rejected the charge. We still owed the partner.

"Another customer complained that the technician arrived late. Our records showed only the time we sent the assignment, not when it was accepted or when the appointment was agreed.

"I want consistent job instructions, spending limits, status updates, and evidence of completion. But these partners work for several companies. They are unlikely to adopt a complicated application just for us, so whatever we use has to be quick and practical on a phone."

## S11. "Our busiest customer might actually be our least profitable one."

*Owner of a 20-person maintenance company with approximately 100 recurring contracts.*

"I can tell you our monthly contract revenue, payroll, and total purchases. I cannot confidently tell you which maintenance contracts make money.

"Technicians visit several customers each day. They record the main repair, but not always the time spent driving, giving telephone advice, collecting parts, or returning after a failed repair. Toner and small components are often taken from a van without being assigned to a customer.

"One large customer pays us a substantial monthly fee, so we treat them as an important account. But they call constantly, have several sites, and insist on the same senior technician. I suspect they absorb much more capacity than we realize.

"On the other hand, a customer with many tickets is not necessarily expensive. Some requests take five minutes remotely. Another customer calls only twice a month, but each incident takes two technicians and half a day.

"Our renewal pricing is mostly last year's price plus whatever increase I think the customer will accept.

"I want enough cost and workload information to discuss scope and pricing sensibly. I do not need technicians tracking every minute, and I do not want inaccurate numbers presented as precise profit figures. I need to understand where the work is going first."

## S12. "When our senior technician is absent, the rest of the company slows down."

*Owner of a 13-person maintenance business that has grown around two experienced technicians.*

"Our two senior technicians have been here for more than ten years. They know the customers, unusual equipment, network layouts, and the fixes that usually work.

"A new technician receives an assignment with a short symptom description. When they arrive and get stuck, they phone one of the seniors. The senior stops their own work, asks questions, and explains what to do. Sometimes they have to travel to the site anyway.

"We have a shared folder for manuals and notes, but it contains old versions, duplicate files, and documents with names like 'final_new2.' Useful explanations are also buried in chat conversations.

"One school has a recurring printing problem that requires a particular configuration. We have solved it several times, but the instructions were never written down clearly. A different technician spent two hours rediscovering the solution.

"I have asked everyone to document their work, but after a full day of visits they write 'printer fixed' and move on. The senior staff are also reluctant to approve a procedure without explaining every exception.

"I want useful knowledge captured from actual jobs, with customer-specific details separated from general repair instructions. Any suggested solution needs enough context that a junior technician can tell whether it applies, rather than blindly repeat an old fix."

## S13. "The technician finishes the repair, but our office still has half the job left."

*Owner of a 16-person service company whose institutional customers require maintenance documentation.*

"Our customers often need a service report showing the device, fault, work performed, parts used, and confirmation from the site contact.

"Technicians complete paper forms or send photographs of handwritten notes. The office then types the information into each customer's preferred report format. Some customers want a PDF, others a spreadsheet, and some require submission through their own portal.

"The same information gets written three times: once by the technician, once in our internal log, and once in the customer report.

"We frequently lack something essential. The signature is missing, the device identifier is unreadable, or a photograph shows the repaired machine but not which machine it is. The office calls the technician, who has already forgotten the details.

"Last month, we completed several chargeable repairs but delayed invoicing because the customer's accounts team required signed completion documents. The technical work was finished; the paperwork was not.

"I want technicians to capture the necessary evidence while they are still on-site, with reports prepared from that information. However, the customer contact is not always available. We need a legitimate way to record that situation and obtain confirmation later without pretending the work was already accepted."

## S14. "We remember renewals only when a customer asks why nobody has contacted them."

*Owner of a printer rental and IT maintenance company with approximately 140 active agreements.*

"Our salesperson negotiates the contract, sends the signed copy to the office, and keeps the relationship with the customer. Accounts sets up recurring billing. The original agreement goes into a shared folder.

"We have a renewal spreadsheet, but it is maintained separately from billing and service records. Extensions agreed by email are not always entered. Equipment may be added during the contract, and the updated terms remain in a quotation or message.

"Last quarter, a customer asked us to collect a rented copier because they had arranged a replacement with another supplier. I thought our salesperson was already discussing renewal. He thought the contract had another six months to run.

"We also approach renewals without a clear account history. The customer remembers every delayed visit, while our salesperson sees only the contract value. We cannot quickly summarize repeat faults, response issues, equipment condition, or extra work we have provided.

"I want earlier visibility into upcoming decisions and a useful account summary before contacting the customer. I do not want a system automatically sending the same renewal offer to everyone. Some customers need a replacement proposal, some need a scope adjustment, and some need us to resolve a service problem before discussing another contract."

## S15. "The customer's head office approves the work, but the branches control whether we can do it."

*Owner of a maintenance provider serving several retail and education groups with multiple locations.*

"One customer has 28 branches. Their head office signs the contract and pays us, but branch managers report faults and arrange access.

"A branch calls about a broken printer. We diagnose it, then discover that the part needs head-office approval. We email the central contact, wait for authorization, and arrange another visit. Sometimes the branch manager has already told staff that we are coming the next morning.

"Head office wants one consolidated report. Branches want immediate updates about their own requests. Our administrator manually rewrites the same information for both audiences.

"We have also had location mistakes because two branches have similar names. Equipment moves between branches, but the asset spreadsheet is not updated. A technician arrives with a part for the device that used to be there.

"Last month, three branches reported similar printing failures. We treated them separately before realizing that head office had changed a shared configuration.

"I need a clearer connection between the customer organization, each site, each device, and who can approve what. We should recognize related incidents across branches while still handling individual appointments. A branch manager should not have to understand our internal approval process just to find out when someone will arrive."

## S16. "Our medical-office customers need fast support, but we cannot interrupt their work whenever we like."

*Owner of a seven-person IT maintenance company supporting small clinics and dental practices.*

"We maintain reception PCs, office printers, local networks, and backups. We do not service the clinical equipment itself, but customers sometimes report every technical problem to us because they do not know which supplier is responsible.

"Most clinics call when reception cannot print or access an application. Our administrator contacts a technician, who tries to determine whether the problem is a PC, the network, or the clinic software.

"The pressure is high because patients may be waiting. However, we cannot simply restart a server or take over a workstation during treatment. We need the right person to approve the interruption and agree on a suitable time.

"Last week, a technician visited for an intermittent problem that had disappeared. He could not reproduce it during the short window available. The problem returned the next morning, and the clinic felt the visit had achieved nothing.

"Our notes often contain too little technical detail, but we also do not want staff putting patient information into service messages or photographs.

"I want better symptom collection, clearer boundaries between us and other suppliers, and a reliable record of agreed work windows. We need to respond quickly while preserving the customer's control over actions that could interrupt their operation."

## S17. "Everyone says the fault belongs to another supplier, and we become the messenger."

*Owner of a ten-person company providing office network, Wi-Fi, and general IT maintenance.*

"A customer calls and says, 'The internet is slow.' Our office assigns a technician. On-site, the technician checks the router, Wi-Fi, cabling, and a few PCs. If the problem appears to involve the internet connection, we contact the provider. If only one application is affected, we contact the software vendor.

"Each supplier asks for different evidence. The internet provider asks for a wired test. The software vendor asks for logs. The customer says they already explained everything yesterday and gets annoyed when we ask again.

"We do not consistently record what was tested, from which device, or at what time. If a second technician takes over, they repeat the investigation.

"A recent issue involved intermittent Wi-Fi problems in a meeting room. We replaced an access point, but the problem returned during busy events. We had no useful record of the conditions when it happened.

"I want a consistent investigation history and someone clearly responsible for follow-up while we wait for another supplier. At present, 'waiting for vendor' becomes a place where tickets disappear.

"I also want to distinguish a temporary workaround from a confirmed repair. Restoring service for an afternoon does not necessarily mean we have resolved the underlying fault."

## S18. "Our warehouse, our vans, and our customer sites behave like three separate companies."

*Owner of a 24-person service business with a small warehouse and 16 field technicians.*

"We buy parts centrally, keep common items in the warehouse, and let technicians carry stock in their vans. We also leave loan equipment and some spare parts at customer sites.

"The purchasing system shows what we bought. The warehouse spreadsheet shows what staff believe is on the shelves. Technicians keep their own notes—or remember what is in the van. None of those records consistently match.

"When someone needs a part urgently, they ask in the group chat. Another technician may have it, but answering requires stopping work and checking. We often place an urgent order even though the same part is sitting in a van.

"Faulty parts create more confusion. Some should be returned under warranty, some should be repaired, and some are scrap. They come back in boxes without a clear job reference. We have lost credits because we could not connect a returned component to its original purchase.

"I do not want a stock process that takes longer than the repair. A technician using a cheap cable should not complete a lengthy form.

"But I do need traceability for expensive parts, serialized equipment, loan devices, and warranty returns. We should know what requires detailed tracking and what can be managed with simpler replenishment rules."

## S19. "We already bought service software. The staff still run the business through chat."

*Owner of a 19-person maintenance company that introduced a service management application eight months ago.*

"I thought our problem was that we lacked a proper system, so we bought one. It has tickets, scheduling, assets, stock, and reports. On paper, it does everything.

"In practice, the office creates a ticket and then sends a chat message because technicians notice chat faster. Technicians reply in chat, and the office copies the update into the ticket. We added another layer of work.

"The mobile form asks for too much information before a technician can mark a job complete. Some fields do not fit our work. At several customer sites, connectivity is poor, so technicians leave it until the evening and enter several jobs from memory.

"Our manager then asks for a separate spreadsheet summary because he does not trust the application's dashboard. The dashboard is incomplete because people are maintaining the spreadsheet instead.

"One technician uses the system properly and looks less productive because his jobs include detailed time and parts records. Another closes everything with minimal information and looks excellent.

"I do not necessarily want another application. I want someone to observe how requests actually move through the company and identify which steps are necessary, which are duplicated, and why staff avoid the current process. Then we can decide what to simplify or automate."

## S20. "The business works because I personally remember everything, and I cannot keep doing that."

*Owner of a six-person PC and printer maintenance company preparing to grow.*

"There are four technicians, one administrator, and me. I still handle sales, difficult repairs, purchasing decisions, scheduling changes, and customer complaints.

"In the morning, I look at yesterday's unfinished work, check my messages, and tell everyone where to go. During the day, technicians call me about parts or technical questions. Customers call me because I sold them the contract. In the evening, I tell the administrator what should be invoiced.

"The business is not completely disorganized. We have spreadsheets, a shared calendar, and invoice software. The missing connection is usually in my head. I know that one customer needs a call before a visit, another has already approved a replacement, and another should not be invoiced until we resolve a complaint.

"When I took three days off, a technician revisited a customer because nobody knew the required part had not arrived. The administrator also held several invoices because she was unsure what I had promised.

"I want to hire two more technicians, but I worry that every additional person will create more questions for me. I cannot spend three months implementing a complicated system while running the business.

"I need a manageable starting point: capture commitments, make the next action visible, and let staff handle routine decisions confidently. After that, I would like to automate the repetitive coordination that keeps pulling me away from sales and technical work."
